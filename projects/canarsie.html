Canarsie market entry

Should a real estate brokerage open an office in this Brooklyn neighborhood?

← All projects

The approach

I combined NYC sales data with neighborhood clustering, local pricing models, quarterly forecasting, and financial scenario analysis in R.

Methods: Clustering · Regression · ETS · NPV

What I found

  • Focus on the core. Standard properties represented 48.3% of the clustered sample, offering the largest initial listing pool.
  • Use local context. A citywide model overpredicted Canarsie prices. Local pricing and neighborhood knowledge matter.
  • Enter lean. The assignment model favored a 4.3% commission and no salaried staff. Returns turned negative in the conservative scenario.

What this means for Canarsie

Canarsie belongs to Cluster 2: Mid-market, the notebook’s higher-volume group. Its 5,955 sales and $268.40 median price per square foot point to an established residential market at a lower price per square foot than the cluster average of about $337.

The luxury cluster sits much higher on the price axis, while many mid-market neighborhoods sit farther to the right. For market entry, the chart helps separate transaction activity from pricing and identify comparable neighborhoods.

How the clusters were built

The analysis keeps residential sales with a positive price and known, positive gross square footage. Neighborhoods need at least two qualifying sales. K-means groups them using four standardized measures: median sale price, transaction count, median price per square foot, and sale-price variability (k = 3; seed = 42).

This chart shows two of those four measures. Sales counts describe the cleaned sample; cluster names are descriptive labels from the analysis. Historical clusters are not forecasts of future prices.
